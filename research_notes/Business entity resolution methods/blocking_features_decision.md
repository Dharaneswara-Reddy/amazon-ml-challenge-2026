# Blocking, Distractor Features, and Decision Layer for Entity Resolution

## Blocking to reach ≥98–99% pair recall at ≤~10 candidates/record, CPU-only, 2M×10M scale

### Takeaway
No single blocker reliably hits ≥98% recall at top-10 in the literature — reported numbers cluster 86–99.96% depending on dataset — so the practical path is what your pipeline already does (union of several cheap, orthogonal passes: sparse-vector top-k retrieval, sorted-neighborhood with composite keys, and a token/IDF pass), each individually tuned for recall, then capped per-record after the union. sparse_dot_topn (Apache-2.0) and MinHash-LSH are both CPU-feasible at 10M-row scale if you shard/chunk the matrix multiplication or tune LSH bands/rows; a 150M-row company-name LSH deployment reached 91% recall on real company data with hand-tuned weighting, which is the closest scale/domain analogue found.

### Cited Findings
- **Sparkly** (VLDB 2023, Paulsen et al.) is a TF-IDF/BM25 top-k blocker built on Lucene+Spark with automatic tokenizer/attribute selection; reported recall across benchmark datasets ranges **86.57%–99.96%** at top-10 retrieval, and the paper's claim is that this simple approach matches or beats deep-learning blockers on most datasets. BM25 also supports fast incremental index updates (no full rebuild), unlike cosine-TFIDF. — [Sparkly VLDB paper](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol16/p1507-paulsen.pdf), [ACM DL abstract](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.14778/3583140.3583163)
- **sparse_dot_topn** (ING Wholesale Banking Advanced Analytics) is a C++/Cython multi-threaded top-n sparse matrix-multiplication library for TF-IDF cosine top-k; license is **Apache-2.0**. Benchmark on Apple M2 Pro over two 20k×193k TF-IDF matrices: up to **6x faster** than plain SciPy/NumPy top-10 selection using 8 cores, with ~40% speed improvement and reduced memory vs. a naive Cython approach in an earlier iteration. For O(10M+) row matrices the maintainers explicitly recommend **chunking into ~1M-row sub-matrices** to bound per-chunk memory and allow distributing the multiplication. — [GitHub README](https://github.com/ing-bank/sparse_dot_topn), [Medium: Yet even faster string comparison](https://medium.com/wbaa/yet-even-faster-string-comparison-d0fb2c7ae7a4)
- **MinHash-LSH at company-entity scale**: "Fast Record Linkage for Company Entities" (arXiv 1907.08667) blocks a **150M-record** company database using MinHash-LSH tuned to capture Jaccard similarity ≥0.8 with >99% probability, using **6 rows × 30 bands**, yielding ~**23,600 candidates per record** pre-scoring (this is a wide net before their scoring/ranking step, not the final top-k). Their RLS scorer then achieves **91% recall** on a Swiss company dataset vs. 57% for naive string matching and 72% for Apache Solr; 89% recall on an accounting dataset; 64% on news-article company mentions (harder/noisier domain). — [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.08667)
- Their scoring function (directly relevant to distractor discrimination too) downweights legal-entity suffixes ("Inc.", "Ltd.") to ε=1/256 since many distinct companies differ only in the suffix; treats diacritics as lower-weight combining marks rather than stripping them; downweights city names embedded in the company name when the city is already known from address context; upweights extracted "short names" 3x; and blends Jaccard + Levenshtein as `0.9·max(scores) + 0.1·min(scores)`. — [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.08667)
- General MinHash-LSH tuning: recall is governed by bands (b) and rows-per-band (r); one study found recall ≈0.99 and near-constant once permutations exceed ~100, with 3-gram shingles giving the most stable recall near 0.99 across configurations. — [arXiv 1407.3191 A Comparison of Blocking Methods for Record Linkage](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1407.3191)
- **Multi-pass sorted neighborhood**: doing several passes with *different* sort/composite keys and a *smaller* window per pass outperforms one pass with a large window, because different keys avoid mis-sorting caused by errors concentrated in any single attribute; MapReduce-parallel implementations of both single- and multi-pass SNM exist. When combining multiple blocking algorithms/passes, the standard approach is to take the **union** of candidate pairs across passes (not intersection), which is what preserves recall (precision is recovered later by scoring/pruning). — [Kolb, Multi-pass Sorted Neighborhood Blocking with MapReduce](https://dbs.uni-leipzig.de/files/research/publications/2012-2/pdf/multi_pass_sn_with_mr.pdf)
- **Meta-blocking** (Papadakis et al.): after union of blocking passes, a weighting scheme scores every candidate pair by likelihood of match (based on block co-occurrence patterns — pairs sharing more/rarer blocks score higher), then a pruning algorithm discards low-score pairs to recover precision while preserving most recall; this is essentially a cheap unsupervised second-stage ranker before the real classifier. **Supervised meta-blocking** extends this by feeding multiple per-pair scores into a binary classifier, or (Generalized Supervised Meta-blocking) a probabilistic classifier whose score feeds any pruning algorithm. Implemented in the **JedAI** toolkit (Java, open source). — [JedAI EDBT'20 paper](https://openproceedings.org/2020/conf/edbt/paper_273.pdf), [JedAIToolkit GitHub](https://github.com/scify/JedAIToolkit), [Generalized Supervised Meta-blocking](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.08801)
- Papadakis et al.'s **ACM CSUR 2020 survey** ("Blocking and Filtering Techniques for Entity Resolution") is the canonical reference organizing the whole space into Blocking (reduce candidate pairs via keys/indexes) vs. Filtering (bound quadratic comparisons via similarity thresholds), covering schema-agnostic vs. schema-based blocking, meta-blocking, and progressive/budget-aware variants. — [arXiv 1905.06167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.06167)
- Practical scale numbers from a production blocking write-up (Zingg): comparisons reduced to **0.05–1% of the full O(n²) space**; wall-clock examples given are 120K records in 5 min on a 4-core cluster, 9M records in 45 min on a 96-core AWS instance, 80M records under 2 hours distributed — these are much bigger compute footprints than a 12-core laptop, useful as a scaling reference point but not directly CPU-feasibility evidence for your exact budget. Recommends a `verifyBlocking`-style diagnostic pass to catch pathologically large blocks (a silent recall killer) before trusting a blocking config. — [Zingg: Entity Resolution at Scale, Part 3](https://www.zingg.ai/post/entity-resolution-at-scale-part-3-blocking)
- **Library licenses**: faiss (incl. faiss-cpu) is **MIT**; scikit-learn is **New BSD (3-clause)**. — [WebSearch aggregation; faiss GitHub](https://github.com/facebookresearch/faiss)

### Inferences
- Given your current 7-pass union already achieves 94% recall, the literature suggests the highest-leverage next moves are (a) adding a genuine TF-IDF/BM25 top-k pass via sparse_dot_topn (cheap, Apache-2.0, proven strong per Sparkly) as an 8th orthogonal pass, and (b) applying a meta-blocking-style pruning/ranking step on the unioned candidate set before capping at top-5/top-10, rather than relying solely on hand-weighted similarity ranking to pick the cap — this is exactly the JedAI/meta-blocking pattern (score pairs by co-occurrence + similarity, prune low scorers) and could recover recall on records your current 7 passes miss without blowing the per-record candidate budget.
- The 150M-row MinHash-LSH company-linkage result (91% recall with heavy hand-tuned scoring atop a wide candidate net) suggests LSH alone as the *retrieval* step is generous (candidates ~23,600/record before scoring) — the recall bottleneck in that system was the scoring/ranking step, not the LSH blocking itself. This implies your 6% missed pairs (Indic-script names, truncated/empty addresses) are plausibly a coverage gap in your composite blocking *keys* themselves (tokens/characters those records don't share with their true match under any of the 7 key definitions) rather than something a cap-size increase alone would fix — a token/character-independent pass (e.g., address-free structural blocking, or phonetic/transliteration-aware keys) is more likely to help than adding more of the same key type.
- No source directly reports ≥98–99% recall at ≤10 candidates/record on a workload structurally similar to yours (short noisy business name+address, 2M×10M scale, CPU-only) — this exact operating point is not validated in the found literature; treat it as an engineering target to verify empirically against your own forensics, not an expectation backed by a citable number.

## Near-duplicate distractor discrimination features (hard negatives)

### Takeaway
The ER literature converges on the same feature family your key questions already name — token-level set-difference features (unmatched/added/missing tokens, IDF-weighted), numeric-field agreement/conflict, and legal-suffix-aware string similarity — plus, in deep-learning ER, explicit hard-negative mining (nearest non-match neighbors from an embedding index) to force models to learn exactly this kind of fine discrimination. Concrete reported gains: importance-weighted Jaccard beat plain Jaccard/TF-IDF by 5 F1 points in one classical study (0.82 vs 0.77), and attention-based learned token weighting (HierGAT) beat Ditto by up to 8.7 F1 points on standard Magellan benchmarks by learning to focus on discriminating tokens/attributes.

### Cited Findings
- Token Blocking's core assumption is that duplicates share ≥1 token; entity-resolution systems commonly extract "one or more non-token variations between identified token pairs, including missing tokens and added tokens" as explicit features, and remove high-frequency (low-IDF) "unimportant tokens" from token-record mappings to focus signal on discriminative tokens. — [WebSearch aggregation of ER blocking/feature literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1905.06167)
- A gradient tree boosting (GTB) classifier using **importance-weighted Jaccard similarity** achieved **F1 0.82** vs. **F1 0.77** for GTB with plain Jaccard or GTB with TF-IDF alone — i.e., learning/assigning token importance weights (analogous to IDF-of-unmatched-tokens) measurably beat unweighted token-overlap features in this comparison. — [WebSearch aggregation](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/users/kripa/smerp2018/samujjwalGhosh.pdf)
- **HierGAT** (hierarchical graph attention network for entity matching) uses attention to give "more importance to discriminating words and important attributes," and reports F1 improvements of **up to 8.7 points over Ditto** (a strong pretrained-LM entity matcher) on standard benchmarks — direct evidence that learned/attention-based token importance materially improves hard-negative discrimination over uniform token treatment. — [HierGAT paper, ACM SIGIR](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3514221.3517872)
- **Hard-negative mining via ANNS**: SC-Block and related contrastive-blocking approaches build an approximate-nearest-neighbor index on bi-encoder embeddings and retrieve the nearest *non-matching* neighbors as hard negatives for training — these deliberately share company names, personal names, surnames, buildings, cities, or address components with the anchor entity, forcing the model to learn subtle identity differences despite heavy textual overlap. This is architecturally the closest published analogue to your "near-duplicate distractor" problem (distractors generated by swapping one word / appending a business-type word / nudging house numbers are exactly this kind of hard negative). — [WebSearch summary of SC-Block / contrastive blocking literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.03132) (full PDF text not machine-readable via WebFetch; summary only, see Gaps)
- Character-based (Levenshtein, Damerau-Levenshtein, Jaro-Winkler) vs. token-based similarity metrics are explicitly framed in the literature as different, complementary error models rather than one being strictly superior: Levenshtein/Damerau-Levenshtein model character-level edits (typos), Jaro-Winkler emphasizes common prefixes, token-based methods handle reordering. A specific reported feature: "predicting 'merge' given an edit distance of 1 between two names" was found useful as a standalone signal — directly relevant to your typo-vs-word-swap distinction, since a word-swap distractor typically has edit distance ≫1 at the character level even though it differs by only one token. — [WebSearch aggregation of record-linkage similarity-metric literature](https://winpure.com/data-matching-algorithms/)
- From the 150M-record company-linkage system (arXiv 1907.08667, already cited in the blocking section above): legal-suffix downweighting (ε=1/256 weight on "Inc."/"Ltd."-type tokens), 3x upweighting of extracted "short names" (the discriminative core of a business name once boilerplate is stripped), and blending Jaccard+Levenshtein as `0.9·max + 0.1·min` are concrete, production-validated feature-engineering choices for exactly the "swap a word / append a business-type suffix" distractor pattern your dataset uses. — [ar5iv HTML](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1907.08667)
- **Numeric proximity for addresses**: outside ER, geocoding literature treats house-number matching as range/interpolation-based (a candidate street segment "matches" if the input house number falls in its numbered range, or is within a bounded distance of the range when exact matching fails) rather than exact-equality — i.e., numeric closeness (not just equality) of house numbers is treated as a graded matching signal, supporting a numeric-distance (not just numeric-equality) feature for house-number nudging. — [ArcGIS Pro geocoding documentation, WebSearch aggregation](https://doc.esri.com/en/arcgis-pro/latest/help/data/geocoding/tips-for-improving-geocoding-quality.html)
- The Kaggle Foursquare Location Matching competition (POI/business-entity matching under noise, ~1.5M records) — the closest public competitive analogue to this task — saw top solutions use **~38–50 engineered pairwise features** per candidate pair, dominated by: cosine similarity of text fields, Levenshtein/Jaro-Winkler distances, Jaccard similarity of name tokens, character-based features, Euclidean/geographic distance, and critically **distance-rank and previous/next-neighbor distance features** (i.e., how a candidate's similarity/distance compares to its neighbors in the candidate list, not just its raw score) — this rank-relative framing is a hard-negative discriminator: a near-duplicate distractor often has a high *absolute* similarity score but a poor *relative* rank/margin versus the true match. — [WebSearch aggregation of Foursquare Kaggle solution write-ups](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/foursquare-location-matching/writeups/re-waiwai-1st-place-solution)

### Inferences
- Your forecasted distractor pattern (swap one name word, append "Ventures"/"Group"/"Overseas", nudge house numbers) is structurally the union of three feature families all independently validated above: (1) token-set-difference / IDF-of-unmatched-token features catch the word-swap case (one token differs, and if it's a high-IDF/discriminative token the pair should score low even though most tokens overlap); (2) legal/business-type-suffix detection-and-downweighting (as in the 150M-record system) directly neutralizes the "append a business-type word" distractor, since "Ventures"/"Group"/"Overseas" function exactly like "Inc."/"Ltd." in that scheme — a hand-built suffix list (allowed under your no-external-data rule since it's hand-written, not looked up) scored with reduced weight should be a strong, cheap feature; (3) numeric-distance (not equality) on house numbers, per the geocoding literature's range/interpolation treatment, catches the house-number-nudge case better than an exact-match numeric feature.
- Given your existing ~45 string features already likely cover raw token overlap and edit distance, the highest-leverage additions implied by this research are: (a) an explicit unmatched-token-IDF feature (sum/max of IDF over tokens present in one record but not the other) rather than only overlap features, (b) a business-type/legal-suffix flag + suffix-stripped comparison, and (c) rank/margin features per candidate within its S1 entity's candidate set (candidate's score minus runner-up's score, candidate's rank) — this last one bridges directly into the decision-layer stacking question below and is exactly what the Foursquare winners report using.
- The GTB importance-weighted-Jaccard (0.82 vs 0.77 F1) and HierGAT (+8.7 F1 over Ditto) results are both evidence that *learning* token importance from labelled pairs outperforms uniform/hand-fixed token weighting — since LightGBM already implicitly learns feature importance across your ~45 features, adding IDF and suffix-aware features as raw inputs (rather than trying to hand-tune a single weighted-similarity score) is consistent with how the strongest reported systems get their gains.

## Decision layer: assignment, stacking, expected-F-beta set selection, prior shift & calibration

### Takeaway
For per-entity F0.5 with an empty-set option, the theoretically correct decision rule thresholds calibrated match probabilities against (half) the group's own achievable F-score — not a single global threshold — and Jansche/Dembczyński give exact and O(m²) algorithms for this; Lipton et al.'s F1 threshold result generalizes to Fβ but no explicit Fβ formula was found in this search. Global-constraint (Hungarian/bipartite) assignment measurably beats greedy one-to-many matching in a real large-scale ER system (~90% vs ~81–83% precision/recall on hard cases), and label-shift correction (Saerens-Latinne-Decaestecker EM, or Lipton's black-box shift estimation) is the standard, well-cited fix for your exact "test has more distractors than train" scenario, provided classifier probabilities are properly calibrated first (isotonic needs more calibration data than Platt but makes fewer assumptions).

### Cited Findings
- **Ye, Chai, Lee, Chieu, "Optimizing F-measure: A Tale of Two Approaches" (ICML 2012)**: compares Empirical Utility Maximization (EUM — train a classifier directly optimal on training data) vs. the decision-theoretic approach (learn a probabilistic model, then predict the label sequence/set maximizing *expected* F-measure under that model). With accurate models and large train/test sets the two are asymptotically equivalent, but empirically **EUM is more robust to model misspecification**, while the **decision-theoretic approach is better for rare classes and under domain adaptation / distribution shift** — directly relevant since your test set has a shifted prior (more distractors per entity than train) and rare classes (5.6% singletons). — [ICML 2012 paper PDF](https://icml.cc/2012/papers/175.pdf), [arXiv 1206.4625](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1206.4625)
- **Lipton, Elkan, Narayanaswamy, "Thresholding Classifiers to Maximize F1 Score" (ECML PKDD 2014)**: for a *calibrated* probabilistic classifier, **Corollary 1** proves an instance with predicted probability s should be predicted positive iff **s ≥ F/2**, where F is the maximum achievable F1 for that instance/group — i.e., the optimal threshold is exactly half the optimal F1 score achieved. For non-calibrated scores, **Theorem 4.1** gives the general rule: predict positive when `b·p(s|t=1) / [(1-b)·p(s|t=0)] ≥ J` (J = Jaccard index at optimum, b = base rate). The paper explicitly states (footnote) that "the results can be generalized to Fβ metrics for β≠1" but the fetched text did not contain an explicit Fβ formula. A multilabel threshold-search algorithm is given: sort by score, sweep the count of positives, and pick the count/threshold pair maximizing F1 — directly portable to per-S1-entity optimal-set selection. **Theorem 4.3** shows that with an uninformative classifier, F1-maximization degenerates to predicting everything positive — a cautionary result for why calibration quality matters before applying this rule. — [arXiv 1402.1892 via ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1402.1892)
- **Jansche (2007)** and **Chai (2005)**, working independently, showed the optimal solution for F-measure maximization over a set of candidates is **either the empty set, or the set of labels/items with the highest marginal probabilities** (up to some count) — this is precisely the empty-vs-nonempty decision your singleton case needs, and directly supports "predict nothing when unsure" as not just a good heuristic but the F-measure-optimal decision when confidence is low. Jansche's evaluation algorithm for expected F-score runs in **O(m⁴)** time for m candidates (naively) — a complexity note relevant only if you go beyond the simple sort-and-sweep approach. — [WebSearch aggregation](http://www.cs.columbia.edu/~jansche/jansche2005maximum.pdf)
- **Dembczyński, Waegeman, Cheng, Hüllermeier, "An Exact Algorithm for F-Measure Maximization" (NeurIPS 2011)** — the GFM (General F-measure Maximizer) algorithm computes the Bayes-optimal F-measure-maximizing prediction set in **O(m²)** time for m candidate items per group, requiring only **m²+1 parameters** (an m×m matrix Δ of pairwise sufficient statistics, plus p(y=0)) rather than the full 2^m joint distribution — this is a tractable, exact algorithm for per-S1-entity optimal subset selection (m = candidates per entity, ~5-10 in your pipeline, so O(25-100) per entity, trivially cheap at your scale) and directly generalizes the sort-and-threshold heuristic to cases where candidate match probabilities are correlated within a group. — [NeurIPS 2011 paper](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper_files/paper/2011/file/71ad16ad2c4d81f348082ff6c4b20768-Paper.pdf)
- **Saerens, Latinne, Decaestecker, "Adjusting the Outputs of a Classifier to New a Priori Probabilities: A Simple Procedure" (Neural Computation 2002)**: an EM-based procedure that iteratively (E-step: apply Bayes' rule using current prior estimate; M-step: update the prior estimate) adjusts a classifier's posterior probabilities when the *test* class-prior differs from the *training* class-prior, without needing labeled test data. Widely cited as the standard "quantification"/prior-adjustment method, still the reference baseline as of a 2021 ACM TOIS reassessment paper. — [Neural Computation 2002](https://direct.mit.edu/neco/article/14/1/21/6577/Adjusting-the-Outputs-of-a-Classifier-to-New-a), [2021 critical reassessment, ACM TOIS](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3433164)
- **Lipton, Wang, Smola, "Detecting and Correcting for Label Shift with Black Box Predictors" (ICML 2018)**: Black Box Shift Estimation (BBSE) estimates the test-time label marginal p(y) using only a black-box classifier's confusion matrix on validation data (no retraining needed), and works even when the classifier is **biased, inaccurate, or uncalibrated**, as long as its confusion matrix is invertible; the paper proves BBSE's consistency, bounds its estimation error, and gives a statistical test for detecting whether shift has occurred at all — useful both to correct for your test set's known higher distractor rate (~23% more noisy records per entity than train) and to *verify* that this shift is real/quantify its magnitude before correcting. — [PMLR 80:3122-3130](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v80/lipton18a.html), [arXiv 1802.03916](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.03916)
- **Calibration — isotonic vs. Platt for boosted trees**: Platt scaling (fit a sigmoid) is preferred with **small calibration sets (<~2000 cases)** because isotonic regression, being less constrained (only requires monotonicity, no parametric form), is more prone to overfitting on small calibration sets; isotonic is preferred with larger calibration sets since it doesn't assume a sigmoid-shaped distortion. Both are standard post-hoc calibration methods reported for boosted-tree classifiers ("Obtaining Calibrated Probabilities from Boosting," Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana). — [arXiv 1207.1403](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1207.1403), [WebSearch aggregation](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/~caruana/niculescu.scldbst.crc.rev4.pdf)
- **Global-constraint (Hungarian/bipartite) vs. greedy assignment in real ER**: "Improving Entity Resolution with Global Constraints" compares ManyMany (threshold-only), FirstChoice (one-to-many greedy), MutualFirstChoice, Greedy (sort-and-skip-conflicts), and MaxWeight (exact Hungarian-algorithm bipartite matching) on IMDB/Netflix/Freebase movie-entity resolution (500K+ entities). One-to-one constrained algorithms achieved **~90% precision and recall** on hard boundary cases vs. **~81–83%** for unconstrained (ManyMany) matching; against 13,005 crowd-sourced ground-truth matches the constrained approach got 98% agreement with only a 1% false-match rate vs. 50% for Freebase's own (unconstrained-style) matches. Notably, the paper found that **exact weight-maximizing (MaxWeight/Hungarian) does not always optimize precision/recall** — at low similarity thresholds the precision/recall curve "doubles back on itself" because maximizing total match weight can force false-positive matches (e.g., matching a movie to a sequel or bonus-material entry) purely to raise total weight; simpler one-to-one heuristics (Greedy, FirstChoice) were more robust across thresholds and across a domain-transfer test (IMDB-Netflix-trained scores applied to AMG-iTunes data). — [arXiv 1108.6016 via ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1108.6016)
- General bipartite-matching complexity: Hungarian algorithm is O(n³) but provably optimal; naive Greedy (sort by score, skip conflicts) is O(n² log n) and is only a 2-approximation to the optimal total-weight matching, but in at least one benchmarking context a Hungarian-style global-constraint approach gave >50% improvement in success rate over greedy matching by avoiding "heuristic path-crossing failures." — [WebSearch aggregation, "A Benchmarking Study of Matching Algorithms for Knowledge"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2308.03961)
- **Stacking / second-stage context features**: the standard stacked-generalization pattern trains a second-level ("meta") classifier on the outputs (probabilities, not just labels) of first-level classifiers plus, in sequence-labeling contexts, "additional information about the original input pattern such as tags suggested for previous and next positions" — i.e., neighbor-context features, not just the single pair's own score. In the closest public competitive analogue (Kaggle Foursquare Location Matching), reported first-stage-to-second-stage pipelines used a first LightGBM stage on limited features to cut ~40 candidates/id, then a second LightGBM stage with an expanded feature set including engineered similarity features plus, per other solutions, **distance-rank** and **previous/next-neighbor-distance** features — i.e., exactly the "rank, margin to runner-up, entity-level confidence" context-feature idea from your key questions, empirically used by competitive winners on a structurally similar task. — [WebSearch aggregation of Foursquare solution write-ups](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/foursquare-location-matching/writeups/2-30-2nd-place-solution-colum2131-part)

### Inferences
- The theoretically clean pipeline implied by combining these results: (1) calibrate your LightGBM's raw scores (isotonic if your held-out calibration split is large — likely true given train scale — else Platt), (2) correct the calibrated probabilities for the train→test prior shift using Saerens-Latinne-Decaestecker EM or BBSE (both need only your held-out validation predictions plus, ideally, a way to estimate or bound the test-time distractor rate — the "~23% more noisy records per entity in test" figure you already have is exactly the kind of aggregate prior-shift signal these methods can exploit even without labeled test data), (3) run per-S1-entity optimal-subset selection via the Jansche/Dembczyński decision rule (empty-set-or-top-k-by-marginal-probability, or full GFM if you suspect strong within-group correlation) using the shift-corrected probabilities and β=0.5, rather than a single global threshold.
- Given your one-to-many structure (avg 3.5 matches/entity, 5.6% singletons) is fundamentally different from the one-to-one movie-matching setting in the Hungarian-vs-greedy study, the direct Hungarian-algorithm result may not transfer cleanly — that paper's core one-to-one assumption doesn't hold for your S1→multiple-S2/S3 matching. However its cautionary finding (naive weight-maximization can force false positives at low thresholds) is a relevant warning against any assignment method that tries to maximize total matched weight per S1 entity without an F0.5-aware stopping rule — reinforcing that the Jansche/Dembczyński expected-F-measure approach (which explicitly allows and often prefers smaller/empty sets) is the better fit for your problem shape than any bipartite-matching-style global assignment.
- Rank/margin-to-runner-up features are supported by two independent lines of evidence (general stacking theory, and Foursquare competition practice) and directly attack the near-duplicate-distractor problem: a distractor near-copy of the true match will often have a high *absolute* score but a much smaller margin over the true match's score than a true match has over its own distractors — this makes rank/margin features a natural bridge between your feature-engineering question and your decision-layer question.

This means ICU-based transliteration is deterministic and rule-based (not a lookup/API call to external data), consistent with your no-external-data constraint if used purely as a local library transform. — [ICU documentation](https://unicode-org.github.io/icu/userguide/transforms/general/)
